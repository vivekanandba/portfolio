I was twenty-four — three years out of a mechanical engineering degree — when Bharat Electronics needed the BMP-II turret rebuilt in 3D for their Futuristic Infantry Combat Vehicle space studies. I bid for that project. I won it. And because I had won it, I was the one who had to deliver it.

That sentence is the whole lesson, and it took me years to notice it. In a big company the person who wins the work and the person who does the work are two people, and the gap between them is where the excuses live. At a medium-scale company there is no gap. You quote the number, and then you go and make the number true.

## Selling before engineering

Before any of the modelling, I had to learn to sell. Not the product — we did not have a product. I had to sell the company and our competence: that a firm of our size could take three thousand legacy drawing sheets of a Soviet-era infantry vehicle turret and give back a validated, moving 3D model that a defence customer could run space studies against.

Nobody teaches a mechanical engineer to do that. You learn it in the room, watching which sentence makes the customer lean forward, and finding out afterwards whether you priced it like an optimist.

## A city where I knew nobody

Then I went to Chennai. Alone.

I had no team there. I had no office there. I did not know who was who. What I had was a schedule, a customer who was watching, and the knowledge that a lot of people had looked at this job and not felt confident about it.

So I built the thing I needed. I had trained at a CADD Centre myself, years earlier, so I knew what a good CAD engineer coming out of one looked like and where to find them. I went to the training centres and hired from them.

Workstations were the next problem. Machines that could hold an assembly of that size were scarce, and I was not going to be issued any. I found them the way you find anything when the answer is no: contacts, phone calls, online listings, until there were enough of them in one room to start.

## Four A0 sheets, and the layers underneath

The drawings unrolled like a saree. Four A0 sheets, side by side. By the time your eye reached the right-hand edge you had lost the detail you read on the left.

They were old Russian designs, and old Russian designs are not flat. They have layers of intent behind them that stay invisible until you actually start modelling one subsystem in one direction — and then a second meaning surfaces, and a third, and you realise the sheet has been telling you something for a week that you only just heard.

There is no shortcut through that. You model, you get it wrong, you understand why the original designer did the strange thing, you model it again.

## The small organisation I built inside someone else's building

I did not run this as a group of freelancers. Inside a vendor's premises, in a city I had landed in alone, I set up a miniature organisation.

I was the lead integrator, and I kept the hardest assemblies for myself — the ones where a mistake would propagate into everything else. The other subsystems went to the engineers I had hired. A set of people did nothing but quality assurance: their whole job was to find what the rest of us had got wrong before the customer did. More modelling ran in parallel back in Bangalore, and models came to me from both places.

To hold five to seven engineers, a QA function and an outsourced stream together, I built trackers — my own project management, because none was handed to me. The resume records the team as eight. It does not record that I recruited, equipped, organised and QA'd it from nothing in a city where I had no contacts.

## The part nobody asked for

The scope was clear and narrow: convert the 2D drawings to 3D models, assemble them, hand them over so that the customer could modify them for their own studies. Nothing in that scope says *animate it*.

We animated it anyway. Traverse and elevation, manual and motorised. The gearing. The ammunition supply moving through its positions. Not decoration — a moving model is the only honest proof that the geometry underneath it is right, because a joint that will not turn in simulation will not turn in steel either.

To do that I had to upskill fast. I was already a strong designer and CAD engineer; showing a customer the *complexity* of what had been rebuilt, in a form they could watch, was a different craft, and I learned it on that job.

It is the single decision from those months that keeps paying. Eleven years later, those simulations are the reason the work can still be shown at all: they are on the [BMP-II turret project page](/work/bmp2-turret/), and when I watched them again while building this site, I could feel the difficulty of each one come straight back.

## Three months, four months

I remember it as three months. The capability deck we wrote at the time says four. My own project record runs November 2014 to March 2015. The drawing count has the same problem: three thousand sheets by my resume, two thousand in the deck.

I am not going to quietly pick the flattering number for either. What I am sure of is the shape: I bid it, I staffed it, I led it, we delivered it, BEL was pleased, and I came home. I turned twenty-five somewhere in the middle of it.

## What it set

I have written elsewhere about [carrying a product from bid to handover](/writing/bid-to-handover/) at Legend, and this is where that habit started — earlier than I had realised until I wrote it down, and rougher.

Everything that came later has this shape. Gadjoy, built on nights and weekends through the years my friends were going out. Products at Sanas that I took from an idea to something enterprises depend on. Every one of them is the same move: say you will do it, then find out what saying that has obliged you to become.

Chennai is where I learned that the second half is the job.
