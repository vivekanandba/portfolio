At a medium-scale company there is no hand-off between sales, design, production and quality. At Legend Technologies, between 2013 and 2018, I had the privilege — the opportunity, really — of carrying a mechanical product end to end. This is what that loop looked like from the inside, and why it matters more to me than any single jig or slip ring I designed.

## 1. Bid

I used to go and bid for projects. Because Legend was an MSME — a medium-scale company — I was authorised to, and I took that ownership: pre-sales, price negotiation, the technical bid, then the technical discussions that convince a customer to hand you the job.

Estimating a one-off jig you have not yet designed is where the loop is riskiest. Every number you promise in the bid becomes a constraint you have to live inside for months.

## 2. Design, with a design-QA gate

Then the design — me as one more pair of hands alongside my team of mechanical designers, while managing that team. Once a product was designed there was a QA cycle for the design itself, before anything was cut.

Two things had to be decided at the drawing board, not discovered on the floor: the production-related tolerances and the GD&T tolerances. We designed for manufacturability, designed for testability, designed for a list of other "design-for" disciplines that I could recite faster in 2016 than I can today.

## 3. Prototype

After the design QA, I took the product to prototyping. A prototype is the first honest conversation between a drawing and a machine.

## 4. Batch production, on the floor

Then production — in batches, because this was the aerospace industry: small quantities, flight-hardware tolerances.

I did not run production from a desk. I went to the floor and sat with the lathe operator, with the CNC operators, the milling operators, the welding operators. I checked on the quality team, whether the quality was up to the mark. Floor time is time not spent designing; it is also the only way a tolerance callout survives contact with a machine.

## 5. Process sheets

Once the drawings were finished I wrote the process documents for each one, so that production and quality could take the part up without me standing beside them. The same person in an individual-contributor capacity and a team-manager capacity at once — I was doing both, and the process sheet is where the two jobs met.

## 6. Inspection and first-article proving

Each batch had challenges I could have personally listed. Inspection came first; then, for most of the tooling, first-article proving — the moment a customer's inspector measures the first part off the line against everything you promised in stage one.

## 7. Handover

Once inspection and first-article proving were done, we handed the product over to the customer. And the handover is where the next enquiry starts.

## Why this is the origin of everything since

I was involved end to end. Bid, design, prototype, production, process, inspection, handover — one loop, one person accountable for the whole of it, at an age when most engineers own one bracket of one aircraft.

That is the reason it gave me so much impetus on my own entrepreneurial journey. Gadjoy was founded in November 2016 from inside this loop, by someone who had already learned that a product is not finished when the drawing is, and that the shop floor is where a design confesses what it really is.
