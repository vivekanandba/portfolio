"If I have to pay you ten thousand, you need to earn me a lakh." My CMD said that to me at Legend, where I was carrying products from bid to handover. He meant it as training, in the harshest way he had, and I took it the most positive way I could: if I am earning a lakh for someone, why not earn it for myself?

That was the genesis. I looked at franchises first — there was a Five Star Chicken and others like it — and racked my mind for six months over what to start and how, working only part-time at Legend while I did. Then I met a man who ran a laptop, phone and tablet repair shop down the road and wanted another branch. We made a deal: he would bring the expertise and the running of the business; I would bring the infrastructure and set up the shop. I put all of my savings, close to two lakhs, into it. I bought old chairs and mixed them with new furniture so the cost would come down.

The premises I rented from my father. He would not rent to his own son — he did not have the trust that I could do it — until my grandfather told him to. So I paid my father rent.

I had always been a designer: I had seen one side of a product and never the life of it after it left. I was a mechanical engineer who thought he should service his own vehicle, and I had tinkered since I was small. Repair was that tinkering, extended into a business.

We opened in November 2016. A couple of days later, demonetisation.

## Six thousand rupees

The first days were exciting and dreadful in equal measure. I would pinch myself that I had started a business; then I would sit for the whole day with the uncertainties. The first month's profit was about six thousand rupees, on two lakhs invested. That was heart-wrenching. Then I began to notice that my partner was bleeding money out of the shop and directing customers to his other branches. I gave him the benefit of the doubt, because I had started with him, and let it run as long as it could.

Six months in, someone removed the roof, broke into the store and stole devices. That was the exit my partner was looking for. Had I let him close it, I would have walked away with twenty thousand rupees, fifty at most. He told me the place was not lucky, that it could not work, all of that. I sent my mother in to see what was actually happening, and what we found was that I would have to take it over. In May 2017 he left. For months afterwards he came back for equipment he had put in — salt in the wound, while I was learning to run a business I had just inherited.

I will still say this for him: he taught me the repair trade. Vendors, suppliers, staff, how a repair shop actually turns — I learned it from him, and I was not going to give up. If I had to sit alone, I would run it and see where it went.

## Ten to eight, Thursdays off

I was twenty-seven. For five years I had been meeting scientists and general managers on behalf of Legend; now I opened the shutter at ten, and when someone came in for a tempered glass — the most basic thing we did — I did it with the same sincerity. I had negotiated with my CMD to stay part-time; he knew what I brought and allowed it. So my mornings were tempered glass and small repairs, and my afternoons were capability presentations to other companies' CEOs, alongside my CMD — [the decks are on this site](/work/legend-technologies/) — and the contrast was total.

The shop was empty in the afternoons and busy in the evenings, so I built the day around that. I opened at ten; by lunch my mother came in; I went to the office until evening; came back to the shop; went back to the office after eight; and stayed late. I kept a folding cot in the store for power naps in the lean hours. Thursday off, Saturday and Sunday working, no travel — I loved travelling and simply stopped. Everyone — my partner, my father, my family — was waiting for me to fold, and I did not want that to be true.

The first month after I took over, the shop made more than my salary: over fifty thousand rupees. Some months after that it made a lakh; some it made twenty thousand. Out of it I paid my father rent, at a family discount I never had to ask for, and my mother a salary. I had my two lakhs back within about six months. In nearly ten years since, there have been one or two losing months. Not letting anything pass by is a discipline, and it is the thing about Gadjoy I am proudest of.

In 2018 I hired my first employee — someone I owed a salary every month, whatever the shop did.

## A book, then a sheet

Devices came in and had to be tracked, so we wrote them in a book. When the book had to be shared between people, I moved it into an Excel grid, one line per device, with every update as a comment on the cell so the history travelled with the row. Then a form for money coming in, tied to the sheet. Then layers: a forecast, last month against this, a break-up by category, and what each device had actually cost me. It began as a sheet and became the way I learned to think in software, in the years I was moving myself from CAD to code. That sheet deserves its own piece.

## Apprentices

Alongside employees we took apprentices at fourteen, after the ninth standard, which is what the law allows. I could pay them less and mentor them, and they were useful in whatever way they could be. One joined in the ninth, finished his tenth, and I steered him to a diploma in mechanical — this was while I was still in mechanical myself. By the time he could go for engineering I had seen the folly of my own path and told him to take AI and machine learning. He finished, he works now and earns well, and he still comes to the store. Of ten apprentices, eight fell away and two went somewhere. I learned who to choose, and how.

## What fifteen thousand devices taught a designer

The first time you open a phone you learn what your own drawings never told you. We bought a magnetic pad because iPhone screws are tiny and each one has a place. I saw that the iPhone 5 through 8 had a bottom casing milled from a block, which is why they survive and why the premium is paid, and I saw how much less other phones were built. I saw brands that curb repair on purpose — screws you cannot reach, parts you cannot separate — and it was not okay with me. A person buying a device is not buying a subscription. They bought the whole thing; they should be able to keep it running, and every device kept running is one less on the pile.

So I learned design for repairability from the other side: where a screw goes so a part comes out and goes back. I brought in aerospace-grade adhesives for laptop hinges — my own concoction — and charged far less than the shops around us; where a hinge had a broken metal insert that everyone else threw away, we brazed it and returned it as good as new. Everyone else put water-damaged phones in rice; I brought in a proper chemical desiccant. I learned the liquid-contact indicators manufacturers hide inside, the radio connectors, and how easily an antenna is damaged by a careless hand — and when the hand was ours, I owned it and replaced the whole component for the customer.

Fifteen thousand repairs later I know how a device is made and what it costs when it is made badly. Design for repair, design for maintenance, design to last: I learned them as a repairer before I could have drawn them as a designer.

## Two minutes

Whitefield is dense with people who run things. The head of NVIDIA, the head of ARM India, a Honeywell vice-president, a former head of Intel — all past those roles now — came in for a cracked screen like anyone else and never announced themselves; I learned to read the demeanour, look them up afterwards, and understand who I had been talking to. What that taught me was the two-minute pitch — how to work out where someone is coming from and convince them of a repair before they have decided to walk out. Every presentation I have given since has that shop counter in it.

## Running without me

In 2020 the pandemic shut the shop for three months. I paid the salaries. My key employee left afterwards; others came and went; the shop went on. For the first five years I put everything into it — time, energy, whatever soul I had. After that it ran on the people and the processes I had put in place. By the time I married in 2024, I was working from home for NovaSignal and came by to look, not to work.

Nearly ten years on, Gadjoy is still open, run by my family, at 4.7 stars across five hundred and sixteen reviews — the numbers are on [the project page](/work/gadjoy/). This is the top of the story, not the whole of it. The partner, the sheet, the apprentices, the hinges each have their own thread, and I will pull them one at a time.
