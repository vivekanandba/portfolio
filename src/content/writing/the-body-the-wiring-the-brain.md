My father bought a computer in 1996, when I was six. It was the first one in our neighbourhood and stayed the only one for a long time. I lived on it: games, utility, films from CD and then DVD and then pen drive, and the *Encarta 95* disc, which I can still see — an encyclopaedia you could click through, with tons of data and multimedia on a single CD. Around 2000 my parents took Oracle courses, and I followed their course material blindly to understand what a database was.

What I never got was the discipline to learn anything useful on it. Entertainment first, a little learning on the side. And then computer science became the subject only the top marks could get into. In 11th and 12th I took biology instead of computer science, and told myself computers were something I could pick up on the side — electronics was the real interest anyway. Engineering admissions sorted the rest: electronics first, computer science second, mechanical last was my preference; mechanical was what I got. My father and my uncle were mechanical engineers, so it was no hardship. I would learn the other two on the side.

I did not, for fifteen years. The thread never quite broke — CAD is a computer; at Gadjoy I read Excel macro code until I could follow it; a Python PDF from college sat on my disk the whole time, unopened and unforgotten — but it never became a skill.

## 2017: a book and a move

Two things landed close together. I read *Superintelligence*, and I watched the AlphaGo documentary and saw move 37 — the stone no human would have played, that turned out to be right. What they did to me was specific. I looked at my own work — the 2D-to-3D conversions, the design iterations, the drawing sets — and saw that most of it could be parameterised, and that what could be parameterised could be automated. I needed to make a serious effort, now.

I had an analogy that organised it for me, and still does. Mechanical engineering is the body: the skeleton and the muscle. Electronics is the wiring — the nervous system that runs through the body. Computer science is the brain. I had built the body and, through Gadjoy, learned the wiring. I wanted the brain, and I wanted to see what would happen when all three were switched on at once. I had no product in mind. I had the thought.

## 2018: tutorial hell

A customer at the repair store, a data scientist working in natural language processing, gave a few of us our first real idea of what data science was. My friends, my first employee and I took a data-science course together; I started on Python alongside it, because I had years of Gadjoy data and wanted to arrange it, view it, turn it over.

And then I spent most of 2018 in what people now call tutorial hell. Course after course — edX, Udemy, Andrew Ng's machine learning course, which I did slowly and absorbed properly and which told me the field was not closed to me. I learned, and learned, and learned, and did not apply. Where I did apply, I got stuck, and decided that more learning would get me unstuck. It did not.

## The side door

In January 2018 I had joined Tech Mahindra — [as a tooling engineer for rolling stock](/work/rail-tooling/), at the client's site. That was the plan, not an accident. No product company would take a software engineer with zero experience; a services company would, if you proved yourself, let you move sideways. So I took a mechanical job inside a software company as a door into software, and spent my evenings on computer science courses so that I would be ready when someone inside gave me a small chance on an AI project.

## Half an hour a month

Late in 2018, a customer's father-in-law came in for a tempered glass. We fitted glass as properly as it could be done, and he sent his daughter, a doctor, who came to the store for months and asked about the projects I was always talking about. I had no idea who her husband was. Eventually she said I had to meet someone who could really help me, and he walked in with two wires to solder.

The wires were for [AirCare](/work/aircare/). He had run divisions of a chip company in India; he was retired, and building air-quality sensors for the neighbourhood. I made him an offer: I will help you with the hardware, will you teach me software? He gave me half an hour a month. I went to his house, sat beside him, and he poured concepts into that half hour until most of it went over my head. He handed me the AirCare codebase and said, see what you can work on. It was a small codebase. It was insurmountable. For six months I went to him, looked at the code, tried something, got stuck, retreated into another tutorial, and came back the next month with my head down. He kept the half hour open. Looking back, it was perseverance he was accommodating, nothing more — I had not yet done anything good.

## March 2020: the registry

Then the pandemic, and [Stop Hunger](/work/stop-hunger/). He needed a system fast: residents registering settlements that needed help, organisations recording what they delivered, so that no street was fed twice while the next went without. I used what I actually had. Spreadsheets as the database. A form on top of them in a day. The pieces assembled in the order the relief needed them, quickly. For the first time he watched me help instead of hang my head, and he saw potential rather than persistence.

When it was over he disappeared for a few months. In November 2020 he called. He was at NovaSignal; I was to join as a contractor. That was my break into code, nine years after I had graduated as a mechanical engineer and about twenty-four after my father brought the computer home.

## What I make of it

The brain was the last of the three to switch on, and the order mattered. I design software the way a person who has machined a part and soldered a board designs it: with a strong sense of what is physical about a system and what is merely drawn. And I learned the hard way that tutorials are not learning — the half hour a month beside someone who had done it, and one real problem with a deadline, taught me more than a year of courses.

AirCare, Stop Hunger, Tech Mahindra and NovaSignal each have their own story; this was the one that runs through them. The raw account this was written from is in my [dated note of 3 October 2026](https://github.com/vivekanandba/portfolio/blob/main/source/my-notes/2026-10-03-cad-to-code.md).
