# From CAD to code — the first computer, the book, the tutorial hell, the mentor, the break

_Spoken to the assistant; transcribed with filler words removed and punctuation added. Editorial
clarifications in square brackets. He named his mentor and his mentor's wife and said in the same
breath that he did not know whether the mentor would be okay with it; both names are withheld here
and in the post until he asks them. He said the chronology is uncertain and may be corrected later._

## The first computer

As a young kid — probably '96, when I was six or seven years old [corrected on reading: 1996, six years old] — my dad got a computer, and it was
the first computer in our neighbourhood. I spent a lot of time on it. I used to play games, I used
to use it for utility; I had the widest exposure to computers when I was very young, right from CDs
to DVDs to pen drives, and watched a lot of movies right from the beginning. We had the Encarta
Encyclopedia 95 CD, which I still remember, with tons of data and interactive multimedia, and I had
all these old games — some of them things you could learn from as well. Closer to the 2000s my
parents went for Oracle and other courses, so I used to just blindly follow what was given in those
to understand about databases and the other aspects of whatever they were learning. I can't recall
the detail, but I recall the fact that I used the computer a lot, and that it was the first in the
neighbourhood for a long, long time before anyone else adopted one.

I continued to use computers later but never got the discipline to learn programming or anything
useful on them. I always used them for entertainment — movies, games — with slight learning on the
side. So I had that initial start, but as time went on computer science became a niche subject that
only the top folks, people with very good marks, could get into. In my 11th and 12th — PUC first and
second year — I had to take PCMB, biology, instead of PCMC, computer science. Right from there I had
the thought that computer science is something I could do on the side; it didn't need the rigour the
other engineering did. I was a little more interested in electronics back then.

From PCMB I had to take B.E. Mechanical, because I didn't get good marks — though I was interested
too. My preference was electronics first, computer science second, mechanical last. Mechanical is
what I got, and my dad and my uncle were both mechanical engineers, so I had an equally good interest
in it. That's how I started mechanical, thinking I'd learn electronics and computer science on the
side.

## The thread stays alive

After I graduated I worked on computers for the designs — the manual designs. Even when I started
Gadjoy, much later, I used a lot of the Excel knowledge I had to start something like programs: how do
we do macros. I started to read macro code and could understand it better, and the interest always
leaned that way.

Right from college I had this very old Python PDF that I always thought I needed to learn. I never
made the time.

## The book and move 37

When I read the book _Superintelligence_, and when I saw the documentary on AlphaGo and saw move 37 —
close to around 2017 — is when I realised I needed to make a serious effort. [*Superintelligence*,
Nick Bostrom, 2014; *AlphaGo*, the documentary, 2017.] I realised that a lot of the work I was doing
as a design engineer could be automated. The 2D-to-3D conversion, the designs I was doing — it could
all be parameterised, it could be automated. That was the vision I started to have back then.

I had this analogy: mechanical is the skeleton, the muscle, the body; electronics is the nervous
system, the wiring around the body; computer science is the brain. I had started with the body and
learned about electronics, but I wanted the mental capacity of the brain as well, and I wanted to see
how to activate all of it. I didn't have clear products in mind back then; this was the thought
process. That's how I started to really lean into computer science.

## Tutorial hell

I had a lot of customers at the store who were computer science graduates, but one person really
invigorated us: a data scientist in NLP, natural language processing. He gave us a lot of ideas on
data science as a starting point. That took a lot of my interest, and I started to work on it with
friends and even with my first employee. We took up a data science course — I don't remember exactly
which, but it was a data science course, I think it's still there — and in parallel I started to
learn a little Python. I was very interested, because at that point I had a lot of data and I wanted
to see how to arrange, view and change it in multiple ways.

For 2018, I think, I went through a lot of what is now called tutorial hell. I always used to take
tutorials — to learn, to learn, to learn — but I never used to apply. Wherever I applied I got stumped
and thought more learning would unstump me. Earlier I had taken various courses; I had even taken
Andrew Ng's machine learning course, and that was another pivotal one, which told me that I could
also do something in machine learning. I did it over the longest of time, but I absorbed it
properly.

## Tech Mahindra, as the door

I joined Tech Mahindra thinking that was my way into software: I knew that in a service company, if
you can prove yourself, you can get transferred sideways — that was my guess — and I couldn't get any
product company to take me for software, because I had zero experience. So I thought, let me start
with Tech Mahindra. I got a job as a tool engineer, but for locomotives, and I had to go to the client
location, Alstom, to work. So I didn't have a really good start for software; but inside a service
company, going to a client location is the highest you can achieve, and I took it. [Tech Mahindra
from January 2018, on the record.] I ran through a lot of computer science courses inside Tech
Mahindra too, so that I could build a reputation to show people there that I was ready for AI, if
they could give me a small chance on any project they were doing.

## The mentor

Then, end of 2018, I think, I met my mentor. [Named in the account; withheld here until he is asked.]
He used to head divisions of a chip company in India. [Named too; the Gadjoy note and post already
describe him, on Vivek's decision, as a former head of Intel who was a customer.] How I met him is
itself an interesting story. His father-in-law used to come for tempered glass. We used to put
tempered glass as properly as possible, and his father-in-law referred me to his daughter, a doctor
[named; withheld], who started coming for the longest time. Back then I was very interested in doing
projects, so I used to keep talking with her about different projects, since she used to ask. I didn't
know whose wife she was. After many months she introduced me, saying I needed to meet someone who
would really help me out, and that's when he came in — with two wires to solder. This was for
AirCare. That's how the journey with my mentor started.

I had asked him: I'll help you out in hardware, can you teach me software? He started to give me half
an hour a month. That's all the time he could spare — but it was golden time. I used to go to his
house, sit beside him, and he used to give me concepts of computer science and the depth of his
working in it. A lot of it went above my head. He gave me the AirCare codebase too, saying why don't I
have a look and see what I could work on, and that's how I got started on my first real project. For
at least six months I went to him. Looking back, the simple AirCare codebase itself was something
insurmountable for me then. I used to look at the code, try to do something, get stumped, and go back
into tutorial hell — edX, Udemy, multiple tutorials — to catch up to what was needed. I just couldn't
do it, because I was actively working in mechanical, by now full-time at Tech Mahindra. [He said
"before I met you I had joined Tech Mahindra" — "you" meaning the mentor.]

The turning point, in terms of the depth of computer science, came when I met him. I'm not able to
get the chronology exactly right — maybe we'll get it later — but those were the pivotal moments: the
book, the AlphaGo documentary, the tutorial hell, joining Tech Mahindra, starting with my mentor, and
slowly, for AirCare, continuously doing work without being able to finish, or to go up and say I'd
done something good.

## Stop Hunger, and the break

During the pandemic I had another chance to work with him, on Stop Hunger. The support I gave him
there gave him the confidence. Before that I always used to go and hang my head in failure, and he
accommodated it only because he saw my perseverance, nothing more. On Stop Hunger he saw how quickly
I could help: I used Excel sheets as the database, quickly wrote up a form, and started working on
different aspects fast. That's when he saw I had potential.

Once Stop Hunger finished he disappeared for three to six months, I guess. In November 2020 he called
and said he was working at NovaSignal and I'd have to join as a contractor. That was my break into
coding.

These pivotal moments ran from 2017 to 2020. I always had this love for computers; this is when I
realised it to its fullest potential. There are branches out of this — AirCare has its own saga, Stop
Hunger its own story, Tech Mahindra its own, NovaSignal its own. I'll stop here; we'll see how to
take up the next stories in the coming weeks.
