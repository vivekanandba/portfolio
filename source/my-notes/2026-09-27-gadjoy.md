## The day I decided

The day I decided was at the previous place, where I was doing end to end. I was told by my CMD
that "if I have to pay you 10,000, you need to earn me 1 lakh rupees." So then I was like, if I'm
doing all this, why not start something on my own? That was the genesis of the thought of starting
my own business. He gave me training, probably in the harshest way possible, but I took it in the
most positive way, and I took it upon myself to start a business. That is how the idea started.

From a young age I was very familiar with computer science and computers; I was very passionate
about computers. Before that I had looked into other businesses as well — earlier, in the same
place, we had a Five Star Chicken and other such franchises; I started off looking at franchises.
Then I deliberated on this for close to about six months. I left full-time work at Legend
Technologies and was working only part-time there. For six months I really racked my mind around
what do I start, how do I start.

Then I met one of my partners — one of the partners who had already started a laptop, mobile,
tablet repair business just down the road. He wanted to open another branch, and that's when I
spoke to him and we made a deal of becoming partners, and I started up the business. He was
supposed to bring only the expertise and how to run the business, and I was supposed to do all the
infra, set up the shop, everything. While doing that I got old furniture, old chairs, and somehow
we tried to make it a combination of new furniture and old so that the cost would be reduced. I had
some amount of savings and I put all those savings into the business, thinking that this is
something that needs to be started now. That's how I got started.

The reason for repair: I've always been a designer, so I've seen one side, but I was not seeing what
exactly the life cycle of a repair business is. And since I was a mechanical engineer, I always used
to think that I need to repair or service my own vehicle; I used to always tinker from when I was
young. So this came as an extension of that tinkering itself.

## The first days

The first month, the first few days, were really, really exciting and dreadful. Exciting in that I
used to pinch myself, saying that I've started a business. But on the other side I had to sit for
the whole day, and I had to deal with a lot of uncertainties.

This place also I had to rent from my dad, and he never gave the place for rent — not even for his
own son — because he just didn't have the trust in me that I could. Only after my grandfather told
him that he needs to give the place for rent to me did he relent. So I had to pay rent to my dad.

The partnership also started to slowly unravel. The first month the profit was a very paltry sum —
very, very less, I think 6,000 or so — after I had invested close to 2 lakhs in the business. That
was quite heart-wrenching to know. Then slowly I started to notice that the partner was just
bleeding money out of this and was directing customers to the other branches. That was something I
didn't have any control over, and since I had started the business with him I wanted to give him
the benefit of the doubt and just let it move as much as it could.

After close to around six months there was a break-in at the store. Someone removed the roof, broke
into the store and stole a few devices, gadgets. That was the exit the partner was looking for —
any exit. In six months he would have given me a paltry sum of maybe 20,000; I don't know, max to
max it would have been 50,000. That was a very hard place I had to look into. Slowly I started to
ask my mother to go and look into the business — what is happening, how it is happening. That is
when we realised that I'll have to take it over. He said let's close the business, we can go our
own way, this place is not lucky, this is not a place that can work out — all the kinds of things
that he told. Instead of just believing him on his word, we were able to restart the business.

I ran with the same brand name for at least the next two, three months. But he used to come and
bleed me dry, saying I need this back, I need that back, I need some equipment back which he had
invested. He said he just wanted out. So it was a salt-to-the-wound kind of situation, where I had
to run a new business which I had just learned, and at the same time deal with him as well.

But I would definitely say that he gave me very good lessons. I learned a lot about the repair
business through him — how to manage vendors, how to manage suppliers, how to manage staff, all
those things — at least from a repair perspective I learned a lot. Based on that I was able to
understand a lot, and I was not someone to give up. I said even if you are not there, even if I
have to sit alone, I will make sure that I'm running this business and I'll see where it goes.
That's how I started.

I started the business with him in November 2016. There's another event that happened in November
2016: a couple of days after we started we had demonetisation, where people were not willing to pay
money, or there were just widespread problems with people spending. [Demonetisation was announced
on 8 November 2016.] That also added to the problem. In May 2017 is when he exited and I had to
take over.

## Taking over

Since he had been running the business, I had been going part-time to my office. I worked out some
deals with the CMD. He knew the value I was bringing into the company, so he agreed, he allowed it
to go ahead.

Once I had taken over I had to open the store on a day-to-day basis myself. We had Thursday off;
Saturday, Sunday were all working. I was 26 or 27 then. [27 in May 2017.] I had to open the store.
For the last five years I had been doing work by visiting scientists, meeting GMs, such big work —
but now I had to sit, and even the person who came to put tempered glass, which was the most basic
service we gave, even that was something I had to do with high sincerity.

Since I was working part-time I had this dual opportunity. In the morning I had to put tempered
glass or deal with people with small repairs; in the afternoon I used to go with the CEO [corrected afterwards: with my CMD, to other companies' CEOs] to give presentations on the capabilities of Legend Technologies — you would have seen the PPT that was
attached. [The decks are in `source/decks/`.] So it was a lot of contrast, which was not very
apparent, and which is quite nice to look at.

Opening the store on a day-to-day basis: waking up in the morning, opening the shutter, sitting and
attending to customers. Mornings not so many people used to come; afternoons it used to be really,
really empty; in the evening there used to be a lot of customers. So it was a mixed bag of sorts and
I had to spend my time judiciously instead of actually just sitting in the store. After a few months
I started to slowly contemplate that I need another person to sit in the store during the off time,
and I need to come only during the time when it is really busy. That's how it started.

The first few weeks, the first few months, were really, really hard. I had no social life, because
we used to sit until 8. Morning we used to open at 10; evening we used to sit until 8; and weekends
I needed to sit — I couldn't go anywhere. It really got to me psychologically, because before that I
used to travel a lot; I liked travelling a lot, and I just couldn't do that. I had to curb myself a
lot to make sure the seriousness was sustainable, because everyone gave up on me — my partner, my
dad, my family — just waiting for me to conk over. I just didn't want that to be true. I persevered
through that phase with a lot of hardship.

The way I used to do it: in the morning I used to open the store, and by lunch I called my mom in.
Once my mom came, I used to go to the office in the afternoon and be there until evening; then I
used to come back to the store, run the business, and then go back to the office in the night,
after eight, to continue work. I used to stay until late in the night sometimes. I had a small
foldable cot put in the store as well, so that during lean periods I used to just go and have power
naps. A lot of difficulties during those initial phases, for sure — a lot of nights, a lot of late
nights, a lot of overnights. I had to balance between work and running a business, and I was just
starting off. I just wanted to persevere, to make sure that the business runs. I just didn't want to
quit.

Then, the first month after we took over the business, I saw a huge profit — probably an equivalent
of my salary, I guess, more than 50,000. And then I had a few more months where I was sometimes
making a lakh. It was all very fickle: sometimes very less, 20,000. But it was all money that I
made, and I had to pay rent, and I even had to pay some amount of salary to my mom as well. Rent to
my dad and salary to my mom — quite a situation. But again, this was much better than trying to get
a business space elsewhere, where I would have been scrutinised a lot more. I always had that fear
of not going and asking my dad to waive the rent. I think they gave me a percentage off what they
would typically take from other people if they had rented it out to them. That was kind enough and
good enough for me to get started on.

Slowly 2017 passed by, and in 2018 I hired my first own employee — not my mom — an employee to whom
I had to pay salary on a regular basis. That was quite a responsibility, because at that point the
business was really starting to fluctuate; it was not always the best business ever. But I recouped
my money, probably in the next six months. After that it has just been a lot of profit. Losses also
— I think I have had just one or two months of losses in this whole ten-year journey. It kept the
discipline of not just letting anything pass by. That is something I'm definitely going to be proud
about.

## Leaving for AI, and the sheet

In 2019 I had to quit Legend, because I wanted to get into AI. [The site and LinkedIn record Legend Technologies as Jan 2013 – Jan 2018, with Tech Mahindra from Jan 2018; he confirmed afterwards that those dates are right and this year is misremembered.] I came to know that mechanical had its
limitations, and electronics, even if it's a business, had its own limitations. So I had to get into
AI. I had seen the progress of AlphaGo and move 37, and then I read a book called Superintelligence,
and then I started reading a lot of books around AI. That's how I got started on AI, and I wanted to
make that shift.

When I was making that shift, I thought I had to make it by going into another service company. I
really didn't want to join a service company after Safran — Safran Engineering Services India, my
first company; I will talk about it when that turn comes — and that was the reason I had joined the
product company, Legend, which had its own difficulties; we'll talk about that later too. But that's
the reason I made the decision to go to Tech Mahindra, the service company: in a service company I
thought there was a higher chance I could shift over to software, which was not readily available to
me as a direct entry. That was my strategy.

When I started to learn about software, I started to use Excel really well for device management. I
had a way of putting all the devices in an Excel grid and tracking them on a day-to-day basis. First
we did it in a book. Then I thought this has to be easily shareable between different people, so I
made it into an Excel sheet, and that sheet had a lot of layers to it: it had comments on each and
every update that came — I put those in as comments rather than anything else — and this was done
on a day-to-day basis. From that I slowly added income: we had a form that people filled once money
came in, and that was connected to the Excel sheet. It started off as a simple sheet, and after that
I added a lot more intelligence to it. I had a forecast; I had what happened during the last month; I
could have a break-up by different categories; and I knew how much income, how much loss, what each
device cost me. All those details I came to know. You can see in our other repo, WealthWeave, where I
have put a lot of details regarding whatever has happened in Gadjoy — how we were tracking it in
different sheets. [Not consulted for this note.]

## 2020, apprentices, and after

Then we had the pandemic in 2020. I had to shut shop for at least three months, I think, but I paid
the salary for the employee. By then I had at least two or three.

Another strategy we took was apprentices. I was taking in apprentices aged 14 — meaning above 9th
standard — because only then did they have the maturity that they need to do something in their
life, and I could pay them less and mentor them; at the same time they could be helpful or useful in
whichever way possible. That was the thought process. I had taken two apprentices, kids who had
crossed 9th — specifically crossed 9th, 14 years old, because that's the law on child labour: we can
take apprentices only after 14 years.

To give a success story: one apprentice joined probably when he was in 9th. He finished his 10th;
then I guided him to take a diploma in mechanical — in the initial stages, when I was in mechanical.
After that I realised the folly of my ways and I myself shifted, so when he had an opportunity to go
for engineering I asked him to take artificial intelligence and machine learning as his B.E. Even
now he still continues to come to the store. He finished his engineering and started to work, and
even after he's working, earning much higher, he still finds time to come to the store. I think we
have mentored folks well like that. I've had a slew of such apprentices and all of them are doing
well, but this one is exceptional because he's still coming, and he just climbed the ladder like
anything. Out of 10, eight probably fell off sideways and two were really good in whatever they were
able to do — two absorbed what I thought they would, made good use of it and went somewhere. The
rest were not so successful, I would say, but anyway I learned a lot about who I choose and how I
choose. That was the personal side.

The pandemic went, and then my key employee left. After that there was a churn of different
employees coming and going, but the business continued. After marriage, which was in 2024, and when I
was in NovaSignal, I was anyway working from home; I didn't do too much store work. There were
already folks doing it — I just used to cursorily come and see it. For the first five years I really
put everything into it, all my time, energy, my soul, into the business. After that it took off; I
had people who were able to take care of things themselves; I had systemised processes, repairs,
everything.

## The technical side

I didn't talk too much about the technical side; let me take that now. During the initial stages I
had a lot of panicky moments, where we had opened a device, some problem happens, and then we had to
own that problem — we used to repair it.

One photo I have where I have all three engineering at the same time: an iPhone opened on one side;
one workstation from my Alstom work, where I had CAD or mechanical designs open; and another system
where I was learning programming, with programs open. All three captured in one picture. I need to
find that and probably post it as part of this musing. [Any screen showing client design work has
to be blurred before it is published — ADR-0007.]

The initial stages: it was all very exciting to open, but then very difficult to put everything
back. Each repair was difficult; there were nuances to it. iPhone screens: when you open them you
need to be very careful with the screws — we got a magnetic pad to keep those small screws intact.
What I got to learn is a lot about how these things are manufactured. Laptops, phones, different
brands — each had its own process. I saw that iPhones milled the whole bottom casing — the old
iPhone 5, 6, 7, 8, before the glass came into the picture — and I've seen how other phones are not
of that high quality. So the premium that is paid for an iPhone, and the reason why it is so
resilient, I've understood.

But then I came to know of different brands and how they curb the repair process itself: they don't
allow easy repairability, and you can clearly see how difficult they make it to get things repaired.
That was again something that was not okay with me. When a person is buying, they're not buying it
as a subscription; they're buying the whole device, so they should be able to repair it and keep it
as long as possible — and this reduces the amount of waste that gets piled up too. So I understood
how to design for repairability: how you place screws, how you place a component that is easy to
remove and easy to put back.

There are so many new things I tried to do, especially for broken laptop screens and the hinges. I
had my own concoction of aerospace-grade adhesives that I was able to bring into the picture, which
no one else did, and I did it for very little compared to what the market or the surrounding people
used to charge. We even used to braze some of the broken hinge pieces — there was a metallic piece
inside; I think no one else did this, they would just use and throw — and we were able to braze
those and get it back as good as new. There are several instances I'm not able to recall all of,
but there were a lot of small technical innovations that really made me stand out, and I started to
pull in very big customers.

The place I set up, Whitefield, Bangalore, had a dense population of big decision makers. I used to meet the head of NVIDIA, the head of ARM India — heads — they used to stay [live] there and come to the store; a VP of Honeywell; an ex-head of Intel. [Named on his decision of 2026-09-27: all were past those roles years ago, so naming the companies identifies no one's current position.] They used to come to the store, and somehow, if I saw the demeanour
and the way they talked, I used to go and check who this customer was and learn about these folks.
They would not come and announce themselves, but I used to learn about them, and that's how I
learned how to pitch. I learned how to pitch a repair to them in under two minutes; I had to
convince them in under two minutes. A lot of that has translated well into me doing presentations —
in a very short period of time knowing what to tell that person to convince them, understanding
where they're coming from quickly, and doing a turnaround. That is again a big learning and a big
opportunity I got. I developed my own network of folks with very high influence. That was another
positive thing.

In technical innovations there were so many more things we did. We saw how a battery bulges. A lot
of other vendors used to keep devices inside rice to suck out the moisture; that's when I got the moisture-absorbent material — a proper chemical desiccant, a bunch of small tiny balls — and introduced that for effective treatment of water-damaged devices. [Clarified afterwards: the point is the desiccant itself, not a product name; others used rice.] I learned how manufacturers put in
indicators, like a litmus test, inner indicators that turn red if there is water damage. Very, very
small details: how the radio connectors are; so many screw-ups that used to happen where we were not
careful removing something and damaged the antenna — then I had to own up and replace that whole
component for the customer. There were so many fuck-ups like this, but we learned a lot from them
and we were able to really become good repair experts. Based on that repair expertise I learned a
lot about how a device is manufactured and how a device can be designed — design for repairability,
design for maintainability, how we design things that last for a longer period, very high quality
products that can be built. A lot of things I learned on the technical side as well.
