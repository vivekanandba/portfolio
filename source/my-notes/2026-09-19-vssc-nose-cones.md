# PSLV strap-on nose cones for VSSC — tooling, production and first-article proving

_Spoken to the assistant; transcribed with filler words removed and punctuation added. Editorial
clarifications and fact-checks in square brackets — he asked for the externals to be verified
("you can check the veracity of whatever I'm saying")._

## The product

The VSSC nose cones are SITVC cones. [SITVC is Secondary Injection Thrust Vector Control; verified.]

These had multiple parts to them. There were sheet-metal parts, machined parts, forged parts, cast
parts, and different materials as well: parts made of aluminium, steel — I think it was maraging
steel — and stainless steel for the top of the nose cone. For all of this, one is we did the jigs,
and the other is we even proved continuous production of this for PSLV rockets.

I still remember machining some of the bottom huge rings that would have supported the side-mounted
motors. Again this was mostly for the roll and pitch of the rocket. They had six strap-on motors, or
I think it was — you can check online. [Verified: PSLV-XL flies six strap-on motors. The core first
stage uses SITVC for pitch and yaw; SITVC on two of the strap-ons is for roll control augmentation —
which is why the deck lists PSOM, SITVC and non-SITVC cones as separate products.]

The bottom portion was an aluminium block that was machined in halves and brought together, and there
was a maraging steel bracket that used to connect this nose cone — and probably the whole structure
below the nose cone also — to the rocket. The top cap, stainless steel, was made with deep drawing if
I recall correctly. All the sheets were rolled; I remember going with rolling templates to the place
and getting these things rolled to the template. I think there were three-cylinder rolling, two-
cylinder rolling.

## Tooling, and what it taught me

First and foremost, I was involved in doing the tooling for this. I learnt a lot about GD&T —
geometric dimensioning and tolerancing — the concentricity between all these sheets, the parallelism
between different sections of the nose cone. There were quite a few other GD&T callouts that I'm not
able to exactly remember; I think surface evenness was one.

On the tooling side we did a lot of work and were able to make a big difference in terms of how to
tool this properly. We had a tool that had different stages to check the concentricity — a centre
tower with an arm, and a lower moving arm that was able to check for concentricity from the centre.
Each one of these brackets that form the inner skeleton of the cone, we had done with a lot of
precision. But while assembly, I've seen the kind of methods and ways in which it was assembled: the
top skin on the skeleton was riveted onto the brackets. So the riveting procedures, the methods in
which the different brackets were placed.

Tools have to be much more precise than the part itself. That's why tool building is a more difficult
task than just building parts — these are tools that have to sustain multiple assemblies, all the
riveting, the hard points, and still remain [within tolerance] for multiple iterations.

## Bidding it

We had a contract with ISRO; I was part of the price negotiating committee. We took the project. We
first did the tooling. The design drawings — the manufacturing drawings for the components — were
given by ISRO itself, but we had to convert it into something that is manufacturable. So we created
multiple sets of tools to make the assembly come true, and other tools for doing those individual
parts as well.

During the bidding stage itself we had to estimate the tooling cost. I had a very elaborate Excel
sheet back then. I didn't have too much of computer science experience, but I had an Excel sheet with
very detailed ways — some thumb rules. The exact dimensions of the components were taken from the BOM
and were fed in with some amount of tolerances for machining or for any other manufacturing process.
We had even considered how to use those sheets to do raw-material sizing, and then cost for different
processes, be it welding, machining, sheet forming, casting, forming.

I was the design lead, and also the person who did the estimation and price negotiation. I had to
write a technical bid and a price bid. All these different Excel-sheet markers gave me a clear
understanding of how close a bid was, and how we could become L1. The technical one consisted of a lot
of design approaches that we had for tooling, for process sheets — all those were also preliminary
documents we gave to ISRO to show our technical abilities. And we had to be price conscious also.

## Making it

We used to have all this machined on vertical machining centres, because horizontal machining centres
couldn't take this diameter.

Some small brackets were machined in our own vertical machining centre; the bigger ones we had to
outsource and get done somewhere else — but again we had to send our quality folks for inspection.
Once quality was cleared is when we bring it to our production place, and we had to integrate it with
the assembly using our jig.

After that we had to write process documents, and then manufacture and first-off prove the components
on our tooling benches. Designing this, then working in the workshop hand in hand with the production
folks, getting raw feedback and criticism from the production folks, iterating on that. Once the
tooling was done, batch production of these cones — and during some of those initial first-off
provings there are a lot of learnings, a lot of things that we would have missed, which we had to
catch up on and update the design drawings for the tool. If at all there were any part-drawing
clarifications we had to get it from ISRO as well.

After it went to VSSC Trivandrum for some kind of coating, it was again sent to SHAR for final
assembly on the rocket itself.

## Working with ISRO

ISRO had a very thorough kind of check. But one thing I would say that was good about ISRO is that
they were seeing how to get the assemblies passed by making necessary changes — whereas other vendors
would outright reject it and we had to figure it out. ISRO scientists were working alongside us to
make sure that some of the part deviations and assembly deviations were addressed, and we were able to
clear some of these parts, or even the assemblies themselves. [He named one other customer in this
comparison; the name is withheld here — a negative characterisation of a named organisation is not
his to publish on my account.]

## SHAR, and what I cannot claim

I even visited SHAR, Sriharikota, when some of the PSLV rockets did fly. We were invited as guests who
could go and have a look. We could see the whole rocket lift off. I don't remember exact launches, but
during the window in which this was done, you can check and see. I had the privilege of going to
multiple places inside SHAR as a vendor guest. And during the bidding process I had even visited VSSC
Trivandrum, giving the ISRO scientists the capabilities that we had.

I think we had built a lot of this for both the Moon mission and the Mangalyaan mission for Mars,
which PSLV had taken. We were part of both of those missions, I guess — you can check and see when
that was. I'm recalling it probably after a decade, so you can check the veracity of whatever I'm
saying.

[Checked, and it does not hold. Chandrayaan-1 launched on PSLV-XL C11 on 22 October 2008, more than
four years before he joined Legend in January 2013. The Mars Orbiter Mission launched on PSLV-XL C25
on 5 November 2013, which is years before the nose-cone tooling project his LinkedIn record dates from
June 2017. Both flew on PSLV-XL, which is the hardware family these cones belong to — but neither
mission can have carried anything from the tooling he led. The post says so.]

I kind of felt really great that I contributed in some small way to Indian rocketry.
